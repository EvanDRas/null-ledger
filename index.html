∅ The Null Ledger

I built two stock-picking models, then tested them as hard as I knew how: rules frozen in advance, dead companies included, real trading costs, outside audits. The answer for both was no real edge after costs. I run them on paper anyway, and this page publishes the results every month, whichever way they go.

What to expect: nothing. Neither model is supposed to beat its benchmark after trading costs. That's my own published, audited conclusion (pre-registered for Model 2, and forced on me by the complete-data rebuild for Model 1). This page exists so anyone, me included, can watch that conclusion face new data every month. When a line runs hot for a while, that is what randomness looks like. My own rules say years of forward months decide, not streaks — so nothing here is a strategy to follow.

Model 1: the S&P 500 ranker

My original system, a monthly machine-learning ranker over S&P 500 stocks: momentum and price-history features, walk-forward XGBoost (no future data in training), top ~30 names, conviction-weighted, 10% position cap, and a sector cap of at most 9 of the 30 names per sector (30% by count — conviction weighting can push a fully-capped sector above 30% of dollars), rebalanced monthly against real trading costs.

When I rebuilt its price data honestly, so it included the stocks that later died and left the index, the backtest's apparent +3.1%/yr alpha collapsed to −0.4%/yr (t = −0.15). Statistically nothing. The exciting version of my model was an illusion created by testing on today's surviving stocks. It paper-trades on anyway, because a published null deserves a live test too.

Model 2: the same machine, aimed at small-caps (“SMALL/clone”)

I cloned the identical machinery, zero re-tuning, into the small-cap band (dollar-volume ranks 1001–2000), where market folklore says the edges hide. It was one of 18 strategies I pre-registered and tested in a gated campaign: hypotheses frozen before any result existed, a build sample (2009–2018) gated by 2,000 cost-bearing placebo portfolios, and a confirm sample (2019–2026) that could be touched exactly once, ever.

It was the campaign's sole build-sample survivor (+1.1%/yr, placebo-calibrated p = 0.0135). Then the one-shot confirm test rejected it on every registered prong. The chart shows the sealed confirm window plus the live forward months, split by the divider.

Every registered strategy, disclosed

Selective reporting is how backtests lie: run twenty ideas, publish the winner. My defense was simple: register everything first, then publish everything. Below is the complete pre-registered slate and each cell's build-sample result under the frozen rules, including the embarrassing rows. Cells ran in two liquidity bands: MID (dollar-volume ranks 501–1000) and SMALL (ranks 1001–2000). Net and gross are annualized excess vs the cell's band benchmark; p is the placebo-calibrated rank against 2,000 cost-bearing imposters. The registered pass rule had two prongs: p ≤ 0.023 AND positive net alpha — the frozen kill rule said any cell whose profits vanish at real costs is dead, however strong its signal.

Why a tiny p can still die: the placebo imposters trade as much as the real strategy and pay the same costs, so beating nearly all of them proves real information, not profit — a cell can outrank 99.95% of imposters while still losing money net, which is exactly what the six "costs ate it" rows did. Disqualified = the cell's placebo null failed a registered validity check (delisting-exposure mismatch) in the anti-conservative direction, so by pre-stated rule it cannot pass — its small p is exactly the false positive the check predicted. The gross column is the campaign's real finding: information exists; costs eat it.

Why these nulls can be trusted

Anyone can publish a backtest. What I'm actually proud of is the process: every hypothesis, threshold, and consequence was written down and hash-frozen before results existed. Every deviation went through a signed, dated amendment protocol. Three independent audits reproduced every binding number bit-for-bit from pinned artifacts, and the final test ran once, under seal, with the null distribution locked before the answer was revealed. The full record is preserved: the pre-registration, 42 amendments, 16 verbatim sign-offs, the audit reports, the hash manifests.

The campaign's one genuinely useful market finding: several signals do carry real predictive information — six beat their placebo nulls at p-values as low as 0.0005 — and trading costs consume all of it. The information is real; the profit is not. That is the oldest result in the anomalies literature, reproduced independently on delisting-honest data.
